# AURA — PRODUCT DISCOVERY REPORT

**Research date: August 26, 2026**

I treated the deleted implementation as irrelevant and evaluated AURA from first principles.

The biggest conclusion from the research is this:

> **AURA should not try to win by being “another AI stylist,” “another digital closet,” or “another virtual try-on app.”**
>
> Those categories are already crowded.
>
> The opportunity is to build an **adaptive personal fashion intelligence system** that connects a person's *real wardrobe, personal style, inspiration, context, experimentation, and actual wearing behavior* into one continuous loop.

---

# 1. Target User

## Initial target

I recommend narrowing the initial target to:

> **19–35-year-old fashion-conscious people who own a meaningful wardrobe, care about how they look, consume fashion inspiration online, and frequently struggle with deciding/styling what to wear.**

Within that group, the strongest early users are people who:

* already care about outfits
* use Instagram/Pinterest/TikTok for fashion inspiration
* have enough clothes for combinations to become difficult
* want to improve their style rather than simply buy more clothing
* enjoy experimenting
* experience “I have nothing to wear” despite having a full closet

### Why not target influencers first?

Creators are a **very attractive secondary segment**, but I don't recommend making them the sole initial market.

Creators have unusually high wardrobe complexity and strong reasons to use AURA, but they also need specialized workflows around shoots, content, product links and monetization.

The consumer product should come first; the creator product can later become a powerful extension.

### Market evidence

Whering currently reports more than 10 million users and already offers wardrobe digitization, daily recommendations, outfit building, planning and social features. ([whering.co.uk][1])

Acloset explicitly offers AI styling based on schedule, weather, color and body type, plus wardrobe management and wear tracking. ([Acloset][2])

Alta combines digital closet, weather/calendar styling and an avatar-based virtual try-on. ([Google Play][3])

So “fashion-conscious people who want AI outfit recommendations” is **not specific enough** to differentiate AURA.

# 2. Core Problem

The obvious problem is:

> **“I don't know what to wear.”**

But that's too shallow.

The deeper problem is:

> **People have more fashion information and more clothing than ever, but very little intelligence connecting those clothes to themselves, their context and the style they actually want to express.**

The user faces several disconnected systems:

**Their closet**

contains clothes they barely remember.

**Instagram/Pinterest**

contains looks they love but don't know how to recreate.

**Their mirror**

shows what actually works on them.

**Shopping**

shows endless new clothes.

**Their memory**

is poor at remembering combinations they've worn before.

**Traditional wardrobe apps**

mostly organize clothing rather than becoming truly intelligent about the person.

This creates the core pain:

> **“I own clothes. I save outfits I love. I know the style I want. But turning all of that into what I should actually wear is still hard.”**

This is stronger territory for AURA.

---

# 3. Jobs-to-be-Done

The primary job is:

> **Help me decide what to wear so I look and feel like myself—without having to figure everything out manually.**

Secondary jobs:

**When I have an occasion**

> Help me create an appropriate outfit using what I actually own.

**When I see inspiration**

> Show me how to translate that look into my wardrobe.

**When I'm experimenting**

> Let me freely build combinations and help when I'm stuck.

**When I'm shopping**

> Tell me whether I actually need something.

**When I'm improving my style**

> Help me discover what looks good on me and expand beyond my current habits.

**When I'm overwhelmed**

> Reduce the decision-making.

# 9. Market Gaps

## Gap 1 — Existing wardrobes still feel like databases

A lot of digital closet software is fundamentally:

> photograph → catalog → filter → make outfit

AURA should feel more like:

> **understand → anticipate → style → experiment → learn**

---

## Gap 2 — Inspiration and wardrobe are usually separate mental systems

People save thousands of outfits but can't translate them into themselves.

AURA can create:

> **Inspiration → style structure → user's wardrobe → personalized interpretation**

This is more powerful than merely finding “similar clothes.”

---

## Gap 3 — Most systems don't fully close the behavioral loop

The crucial difference is:

> recommendation ≠ reality

AURA should learn what happened after the recommendation.

Example:

> Suggested beige trousers
> User replaced them with black
> User saved the look
> User wore it
> User uploaded a photo
> User repeated it later

That is exceptionally valuable personalization data.

This should become a core product thesis.

---

## Gap 4 — User agency

Many AI products risk becoming:

> “Here is what the algorithm thinks you should wear.”

Your Mix & Match idea creates a better relationship:

> **AURA proposes. You create. AURA learns.**

This is potentially a major UX advantage.

---

## Gap 5 — Wardrobe utilization

Indyx and Whering already recognize unused wardrobe value, so this is not unique by itself. ([Indyx][5])

But AURA can make it much more actionable:

> **“You have 8 outfits you haven't worn yet that fit your current style.”**

That is much more useful than a wardrobe analytics dashboard.

# 22. VTO Recommendation

This was one of your explicit research questions.

## What exists today?

Image-based diffusion VTO has improved dramatically.

IDM-VTON specifically targets garment identity preservation, addressing a major weakness of earlier approaches. ([arXiv][8])

CatVTON demonstrated a simpler VTO architecture with much lower inference requirements than some earlier systems. ([arXiv][9])

More recent work continues improving efficiency and fidelity, such as Re-CatVTON. ([arXiv][10])

Commercial systems already expose VTO APIs. FASHN, for example, offers commercial VTO APIs and its recent model accepts person images plus garment images without requiring conventional segmentation masks. ([GitHub][11])

## My recommendation

### Do NOT start with a 3D avatar.

For the core AURA experience, a high-quality **image-based VTO pipeline** is currently the more rational starting point.

Why?

Because the user primarily wants:

> **“Show me what I look like wearing this outfit.”**

They don't necessarily care whether the system internally reconstructed a perfect 3D garment.

### 2D image-based VTO wins initially on:

* implementation complexity
* time to market
* model/provider choice
* cost
* image quality
* replaceability

### 3D should remain an R&D path

especially if AURA eventually needs:

* arbitrary viewing angles
* interactive rotation
* persistent avatars
* live try-on
* complex layering
* garment physics

Research such as VTON 360 shows that multi-view/3D-consistent VTO is an active research problem rather than a solved commodity capability. ([arXiv][12])

### Therefore:

**VTO belongs in the AURA vision.**

But:

> **VTO is not the product.**

AURA should still be valuable without it.

# 23. Real Garment Problem

This is one place where I strongly agree with your original insistence.

AURA must distinguish between:

**Original garment**

↓

**clean representation**

↓

**semantic garment understanding**

↓

**VTO conditioning**

Those are not the same artifact.

We should never claim:

> “We reconstructed your actual 3D shirt”

when what actually exists is a generated approximation.

Commercial and research VTO systems still rely heavily on the quality and nature of person/garment inputs; current systems explicitly support different garment image types and make trade-offs around fidelity and efficiency. ([arXiv][8])

So the product architecture should preserve the **source of truth: the original garment photo**.

**Height + weight + body understanding**

→ proportions / fit

**Best outfit photos**

**Full-body reference**

**Style/outfit references**

The emotional message should be:

* face imagery
* body imagery
* location

* delete images

# 27. Product Principles

After the research and interview, I would keep these:

### REAL CLOTHES > GENERIC CLOTHING

The user's actual wardrobe remains the source of truth.

### PERSONAL CONTEXT > GENERIC RECOMMENDATIONS

“Tonight's dinner” beats “here's a fashionable outfit.”

### AI SUGGESTS > USER CONTROLS

The user remains the stylist.

### BEHAVIOR > QUESTIONNAIRES

AURA learns through actions wherever possible.

### VISUAL > EXPLANATORY

Show the user the result before explaining the algorithm.

### INVISIBLE INTELLIGENCE > AI DASHBOARD

Users should feel AURA getting smarter without managing a “Style DNA” system.

### BUY BETTER > BUY MORE

Shopping is downstream from styling.

### EVOLVE STYLE > PREDICT STYLE ONLY

AURA should help users discover new versions of themselves.

### TRUST > ENGAGEMENT

No fake intelligence, fake confidence or manipulation.

### USER VALUE > TECHNICAL COMPLEXITY

3D exists only if it improves the experience.

# 30. Product Definition — Proposed

### Who is AURA for?

Fashion-conscious young adults who want to improve how they dress and make better use of the clothes they already own.

### What problem?

They struggle to transform their wardrobe, inspiration and personal preferences into outfits they actually want to wear.

### Why is AURA different?

It builds a continuously learning understanding of **the person's real fashion life**, rather than merely generating outfits.

### Why download?

> **“AURA can understand my actual wardrobe and help me figure out what to wear.”**

### Why return tomorrow?

Because AURA becomes increasingly relevant to the user's actual life and context.

### Why does it get better?

Because it learns from:

> what you own + what you like + what you change + what you save + what you wear.

### Hero experience?

**Contextual AURA styling → personalized outfit → Mix & Match → personal visualization → save/wear → learn.**

### Long-term ambition?

> **Make fashion easier and help people become better at dressing.**

# 31. My Biggest Strategic Warning

There is one thing I would **not** do.

Do not try to launch AURA as:

> **Home + Closet + AI Stylist + Mix & Match + VTO + Shopping + Social + Creator tools + Trend engine + Calendar + Analytics**

all at once.

The research makes this especially important because competitors already have pieces of almost every one of those capabilities. ([whering.co.uk][1])

AURA needs to win through **how those capabilities work together**, not by having the longest feature list.

---

# 32. The Proposed MVP Thesis

My recommendation is therefore:

> **AURA should first prove that a user can give it their real wardrobe, receive a genuinely personal outfit, take control of that outfit through visual Mix & Match, and have AURA become measurably better from their behavior.**

Everything else should support that.

VTO is valuable.

Inspiration is valuable.

Creator tools are valuable.

Shopping is valuable.

But they should orbit this central loop rather than compete with it.
